## 1. How to read this report
- **Confidence interval (CI):** a range that very likely holds the true value. A narrow CI means a precise estimate.
- **p-value:** how surprising the data would be if there were no real effect. Small means surprising.
- **Effect size:** how big the difference is. A tiny effect can still have a tiny p-value, so we always show both.
- **Multiple testing:** we ran 34 tests, so a few would look significant by luck alone. The **Holm correction** adjusts every p-value for this. **Significant** below means it survives that correction.

## 3. Quality
- **Yield** averages 88.6% (95% CI 88.1% to 89.1%). Production lines, batch status and time trend show no differences (p = 0.617, 0.729, 0.143).
- Yield differs strongly **between products** (eta-squared = 0.32), but this is expected because each product has its own target. Against those targets the average gap is 0.17 (95% CI -0.22 to 0.57) points, which includes zero (p = 0.384).
- **Control chart:** with limits from batch-to-batch variation, 0 of 465 batches fall outside 73.3% to 100.0% (the upper limit is capped at 100%), and the batch-to-batch correlation is 0.01. The process is **statistically stable**: yield varies randomly around 88.6% with no signal to investigate.
- **Review rate** is 29.7% (95% CI 25.7% to 34.0%). By product and by line the differences are not significant (p = 0.490 and 0.361), but with only about 35 batches per product a test could detect only differences of about 22 points or more. So "no significant difference" here means **inconclusive for small differences**, not proof that products are equal.
- **Lab measurements need the right quantity.** Raw pH is only nominally different between Review and Pass batches (p = 0.031, d = 0.27, adjusted p = 0.839), and the other raw values look the same, because errors in both directions cancel in averages. But the **ABV gap from the product's declared ABV** is much larger in Review batches (d = 0.80, p <0.0001). The rule "ABV gap above 0.30 or pH outside 4.1 to 5.0" reproduces the real status for **99.4%** of batches (135 flagged, 138 in Review; 0 false alarms, 3 missed). The thresholds were found by looking at the data, so confirm them with the brewery.
- **Early warning is weak:** a batch is not more likely to be Review because the previous batch on its line was (p = 0.676, Cramer's V = 0.02).

## 4. Supply
- **57.2% (95% CI 54.4% to 60.0%)** of deliveries are late.
- Supplier differences (p = 0.090) and the 2023 vs later improvement (p = 0.070) are **not significant**, so the EDA statement that lateness "improved after 2023" is not statistically supported.
- A supplier's stated on-time rate does not predict its real one (Spearman rho = -0.06, p = 0.808).

## 5. Profitability
- **Gross margin** is 66.0% (95% CI 65.9% to 66.1%) overall (bootstrap over orders). Product margins are very precise: Velora Classic Lager 59.7% (CI 59.4 to 59.9) up to Alpine IPA 69.7% (CI 69.5 to 69.9). These ranges do not overlap, so the gap is real.
- **Margin follows price:** each extra EUR 1 of wholesale price adds 12.2 (95% CI 7.2 to 17.2) points of margin (r = 0.85, R-squared = 0.72, based on 13 products).
- **Discounts cost margin:** each extra point of discount changes margin by -0.39 (95% CI -0.40 to -0.37) points. This is partly arithmetic (revenue falls while cost stays).
- **Discounts do not bring volume:** each extra point of discount changes units per line by -0.3 (95% CI -1.8 to 1.2) units (p = 0.720). The upper end of the interval means a 10-point discount could raise a typical order (about 627 units) by at most about 2%, far below the roughly 11% volume increase a 10% discount needs just to keep revenue level.
- Margin does not differ by sales channel (p = 0.207). Cancellations are 17.0% (95% CI 15.9% to 18.1%) with no difference by channel or year.

## 6. Sales, seasonality and demand
- **Sales are flat:** Jan-Oct monthly sales do not differ between years (p = 0.991) and show no trend over 46 months (p = 0.694).
- **Sales seasonality is inconclusive:** effect size 0.42 is large, but with 3 to 4 observations per calendar month the test cannot decide (Kruskal p = 0.077; ANOVA p = 0.039).
- **Total demand is clearly seasonal:** after removing the trend, calendar month explains 71% of the variation (p = 0.0007). Seasonal index (percent of an average month): Jan +20%, Feb +12%, Mar +10%, Apr +10%, May +6%, Jun -1%, Jul -3%, Aug -10%, Sep -23%, Oct -18%, Nov -9%, Dec +6%.
- **Demand is trending up:** 257 (95% CI 136 to 377) units more per month, month after month, seasonally adjusted (p = 0.0001). A trend test without seasonal adjustment misses this (p = 0.13) because the seasonal swings hide it.
- **Individual products are too noisy to show it alone:** only 1 of 13 products shows a significant calendar-month effect on their own, and products move together very little (average correlation between product series 0.09). Pooled across all product-months the seasonal pattern still correlates with units sold (rho = 0.29, p <0.0001), so it is real but small per product.
- Promotions do not raise units sold (rho = 0.06, p = 0.127).

## 7. What this means for the machine learning steps
1. **QC status: two different problems.** (a) *Classifying* Review from the QC measurements will be almost perfect, because Review is a specification check; a model only rediscovers the rule, so always compare it with the two-line rule and say so in the report. (b) *Predicting Review before the test* from pre-test information (product, line, volume, previous batch outcomes) has little to learn from.
2. **Product-level demand forecasts will be noisy.** Seasonality and trend are real in the total, but weak per product. Compare models against simple baselines (product average, seasonal index) before claiming improvement.
3. **Useful features:** `abv_gap` and pH window for QC; calendar month (or seasonal index), a time trend and price tier for demand. Promotion discount adds little.
4. **Do not model supplier or line differences** for yield or lateness; the data gives no evidence they exist.
